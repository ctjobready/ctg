## The Question That Stayed with Me

A few days ago, I published an essay titled “The Father, the Godfather, and the Jobs of Our Children and Youth,” reflecting on the warnings of two pioneers of artificial intelligence: John McCarthy, who gave the field its name, and Geoffrey Hinton, whose work helped make modern AI possible.

My central concern was straightforward: What happens to millions of young people worldwide when artificial intelligence begins performing the very jobs we have spent decades preparing them to do? Bangladesh, where CodersTrust has built a substantial training operation, offers an especially revealing case study.

The essay generated a thoughtful conversation on Facebook. One response, from my friend Sam G. Samdani, challenged me to think beyond the immediate problem of skills and employment. He introduced a concept that deserves much greater attention: affordance literacy.

Samdani raised a fundamental distinction. Are we simply preparing young people for existing jobs, many of which may disappear, or are we enabling them to imagine and create entirely new forms of work and economic value? He asked how we could ensure that human opportunity expands faster than AI’s capacity to replace human labor.

That question has stayed with me. It connects my earliest experiences building telecommunications infrastructure in the United States with my work over the past twelve years helping young people develop digital skills through CodersTrust.

The next great educational challenge is not merely teaching young people how to use technology. It is teaching them how to recognize what technology makes possible—and how to turn those possibilities into meaningful economic opportunity.

## What Is Affordance Literacy, and Why Does It Matter?

The concept of affordance has a long intellectual history. Psychologist James J. Gibson developed the idea to describe the possibilities for action that an environment offers to an individual. Designer Donald Norman later helped popularize the concept in human-centered design.

A chair may afford sitting, a staircase climbing, and a door passage, but the possibilities depend on the relationship between a person’s capabilities and the environment. An affordance may exist even when nobody notices it. Three questions matter: What does this environment afford this person? Can the person perceive those possibilities? And can the person mobilize the capabilities and resources needed to act?

Technology works similarly. The internet affords communication, education, commerce, collaboration, and entrepreneurship. Artificial intelligence affords analysis, automation, design, discovery, and entirely new ways of solving problems. Yet access to these technologies does not automatically translate into opportunity.

Two young people can possess the same smartphone, internet connection, and AI tools, but experience dramatically different economic outcomes. One may use the technology primarily to consume information and entertainment. The other may identify an underserved market, develop a service, reach customers internationally, and establish a sustainable business.

I propose affordance literacy as an applied educational framing: learning to perceive, evaluate, and act upon possibilities arising from the relationship between our capabilities and our environments. This is distinct from Gibson’s established ecological concept itself. Affordance literacy includes curiosity, judgment, experimentation, and the capacity to reshape environments and collaborate so that new possibilities become available.

Digital literacy teaches us how to use a tool. AI literacy teaches us how to understand, evaluate, and work responsibly with intelligent systems. Affordance literacy asks a further question: What can I make possible with these capabilities that was previously difficult, expensive, or unimaginable?

Affordances extend well beyond income and entrepreneurship. They include scientific discovery, learning, relationships, creativity, civic participation, and community life. This essay deliberately concentrates on education and economic empowerment, but the underlying capacity to recognize and develop possibilities can enrich how we live and engage with one another.

## A Lesson I Learned at AT&T Three Decades Ago

As a lead architect involved in AT&T’s 21st-century network initiatives and a participant in discussions surrounding the Next Generation Internet, I witnessed an extraordinary technological transition in the 1990s.

We were helping build communications infrastructure that would transform the movement of information across businesses, institutions, and societies. At the time, much of the conversation revolved around networks, bandwidth, switching, protocols, reliability, and connectivity. Those were essential engineering challenges.

But the most profound economic transformation did not come from the infrastructure alone. It came from what people imagined they could build upon it. The internet made possible electronic commerce, cloud computing, online education, digital marketplaces, remote employment, financial technology, and entire industries that had scarcely existed.

The engineers built networks. Entrepreneurs, educators, researchers, and millions of ordinary people discovered new possibilities within those networks. Infrastructure creates potential. Human imagination converts potential into opportunity.

Today, artificial intelligence represents another foundational technological transition. We are again investing heavily in infrastructure: data centers, computing power, algorithms, models, connectivity, and software. But the question that should concern us most is whether our people can recognize and use the opportunities that technology creates.

## Bangladesh as a Case Study of a Global Challenge

Bangladesh has made meaningful progress in digital connectivity, education, and technology adoption. Yet a painful contradiction remains. We have large numbers of educated young people seeking work while employers struggle to find particular capabilities, and many communities lack the economic institutions needed to create sustainable employment.

The Bangladesh Labour Force Survey 2024 recorded approximately 2.62 million unemployed people, including more than two million between the ages of 15 and 29. University graduates face especially serious employment challenges.

Now AI is reshaping entry-level jobs. Routine content creation, basic graphic design, data entry, administrative processing, and repetitive software tasks are increasingly vulnerable to automation. As Geoffrey Hinton has warned, AI poses a serious threat to routine intellectual labor.

But replacing routine tasks is only one side of the equation. AI also reduces the cost of creating products, analyzing markets, developing software, serving customers, and operating businesses. A small company in Chattogram can potentially serve customers in London. A student in Rajshahi can build a software prototype without a large engineering team.

These are possibilities, not guaranteed outcomes. Without customers, capital, mentorship, trust, and functioning markets, opportunities remain theoretical. We must stop treating skills development as a supply-only exercise. We must also create demand for those skills.

Training a million people without developing markets, enterprises, and pathways to employment risks producing a million disappointed certificate holders. Our objective must be to make young people economically capable—able to secure employment, create businesses, develop services, solve problems, and generate value for others.

## What Twelve Years of CodersTrust Have Taught Me

When my late friend Ferdinand Kjærulff and I co-founded CodersTrust in 2014, we were motivated by a simple belief: Talent is widely distributed. Opportunity is not.

Over the past twelve years, CodersTrust has trained more than 130,000 young people across programs serving 15 countries. Training has real value only when it improves people’s ability to participate in economic life.

The evaluation’s Table 4 reports treatment-on-treated estimates of approximately 25.75% higher employment and 54.56% higher monthly income for participants who received training, relative to the relevant comparison estimates. These figures describe the evaluated women’s program, not all CodersTrust learners or all settings; they should not be confused with intention-to-treat estimates.

Among women who attended training, the evaluation estimated approximately 53% higher monthly income and 28% higher employment relative to the control group. The study also identified improvements in household expenditure, savings, and other measures of well-being.

Our October 2026 Impact Report offers additional evidence, while reminding us why rigorous measurement is essential. Among responding course completers, reported employment increased from 47.4% before training to 77.9% at the time of the survey. Median monthly income rose from approximately $82 to $245. Among respondents from the 2014–2023 completion cohorts who reported no income before training, 72.3% were earning at the time of the survey.

For transparency, the 2026 CodersTrust impact report’s alumni figures describe respondents, not a randomized comparison group. Its response rate, cohort definitions, and questionnaire should accompany any public interpretation of those figures; absent that information here, they should not be treated as population-wide causal estimates.

These are encouraging findings. However, unlike the independent randomized trial, the alumni survey is self-reported and descriptive. It cannot establish that training alone caused every improvement.

Will the skills that helped our graduates earn during the past decade remain economically valuable in the next? If AI can perform tomorrow what we teach today, we have a responsibility to redesign our approach. CodersTrust must continue evolving—from teaching individual digital tasks toward developing AI-enabled problem-solvers, entrepreneurs, and professionals who can identify opportunities in changing markets.

The purpose of sharing these Bangladesh findings is not to suggest that every country should copy CodersTrust unchanged. It is to identify a transferable model: provide access, build relevant digital and AI capabilities, teach people to recognize market opportunities, connect them with customers and employers, and measure whether livelihoods improve. Each implementation must be redesigned with local partners, language, infrastructure, financing and labor-market realities in mind.

## From Digital Access to Digital Capabilities—and Economic Opportunity

I believe digital empowerment evolves through three connected stages.

The first stage is digital access: affordable connectivity, reliable electricity, appropriate devices, and digital services. Without access, exclusion is almost inevitable.

The second stage is digital capability: using technology effectively, critically, safely, and productively, with technical skills, communication abilities, analytical thinking, digital confidence, and AI literacy.

The third stage is opportunity creation: identifying problems worth solving, understanding market demand, building relationships, mobilizing resources, developing products or services, and translating capabilities into sustainable economic outcomes.

Consider a young woman in rural Bangladesh—a situation with parallels in communities from Kenya to the Philippines and from small-town America to emerging economies. An internet connection creates access. Teaching digital marketing or AI-assisted content development creates capability. Helping her identify customers, validate a business need and earn sustainably creates opportunity. The sequence is globally relevant; the practical support required will differ by place.

In the first stage, she becomes connected. In the second, she becomes skilled. In the third, she becomes economically empowered. Our education systems should aspire to deliver all three.

## AI Literacy Is Necessary. Affordance Literacy Takes Us Further.

Affordance literacy is not a replacement for existing UNESCO or OECD–European Commission AI literacy frameworks, which already address ethical use, creativity, and designing with AI. I see it as a complementary, action-oriented extension: the ability to notice what an environment permits, work with others to enlarge those possibilities, and test whether a promising idea becomes a responsible and useful reality.

UNESCO’s AI Competency Framework for Students emphasizes human-centered thinking, ethics, AI applications, and system design. The OECD and European Commission’s 2026 AI Literacy Framework emphasizes engaging with, creating with, managing, and designing AI responsibly.

A possible five-stage learning cycle could make this idea teachable and testable. OBSERVE: map local resources, constraints, people, institutions, and technologies. DISCOVER: identify overlooked possibilities. COMBINE: connect capabilities, disciplines, collaborators, and AI tools to create additional possibilities. EXPERIMENT: test usefulness, feasibility, demand, and responsible use. ACTUALIZE: turn a promising possibility into a sustainable service, better practice, discovery, or community outcome. This is a proposed framework, not a validated CodersTrust curriculum. Its incremental value should be evaluated against conventional digital and AI training.

Not every learner needs to found a company. A nurse might use AI with clinical judgment to improve patient communication; a teacher could redesign feedback for diverse learners; an engineer could combine local expertise with AI to improve maintenance; a public servant could make services more accessible. Human–AI complementarity can improve existing professions as well as create new ones.

These developments acknowledge that students must become more than passive consumers of intelligent technologies. But we should go further: teach students where their creations can generate human and economic value.

Imagine a university course where students identify a real community problem, interview potential users, examine solutions, build a prototype, test usefulness, and demonstrate a plausible path to adoption or revenue.

An agriculture student might develop a crop advisory service. A nursing student might design a multilingual patient education tool under clinical supervision. An engineering student might create a maintenance application for small manufacturers. In each case, technology becomes a means rather than an end.

Educational ambition alone is insufficient. A learner may clearly recognize an opportunity but lack capital, connectivity, safe transportation, institutional support, or time because of caregiving responsibilities; discrimination may close doors as well. Education can strengthen the ability to perceive and pursue possibilities, while employers, governments, and institutions must expand the possibilities that are genuinely accessible.

## Universities Must Become Opportunity-Creation Institutions

For too long, the relationship between education and employment has followed a simple sequence: attend classes, earn a degree, apply for a job. That model was already under pressure before AI.

Universities must prepare students for rapidly changing job descriptions, emerging industries, and entrepreneurial initiative. Students should leave with portfolios, demonstrated problem-solving abilities, professional networks, practical experience, and an understanding of how to identify opportunities.

Bangladesh’s National University system offers one important opportunity to test work-oriented, AI-enabled learning at scale. CodersTrust’s growing collaboration with National University can generate lessons for universities and vocational institutions elsewhere. The ambition is not to export a fixed Bangladesh curriculum, but to share an adaptable approach: start with local demand, teach relevant capabilities, connect learners to real opportunities, and measure lasting outcomes.

Scale alone must never define success. We should ask how many students become economically active, build sustainable enterprises, obtain meaningful employment, and create opportunities for others.

## The Missing Ingredient: Creating Demand, Not Just Supplying Talent

Skills policy and economic development policy cannot operate separately. Governments must encourage new markets, support small and medium enterprises, modernize public services, strengthen export-oriented digital industries, and make it easier for entrepreneurs to grow businesses.

Employers must help design curricula and offer real projects, internships, and apprenticeships. Technology companies must make advanced tools accessible while investing in responsible local adoption. Financial institutions must develop mechanisms for credible young entrepreneurs who lack traditional collateral.

Properly organized, diaspora relationships can do more than connect existing supply with demand: they can generate new affordances. A learner, an international customer, a researcher, a domain expert, and an AI system may together accomplish something none could deliver independently. Structured cross-border projects can turn trust and collaboration into shared infrastructure for opportunity.

There is a difference between training 10,000 young people in AI-assisted software development and helping them identify genuine problems for which software solutions are needed. The first approach produces technical capacity. The second can potentially produce businesses, products, services, and employment. We need both.

In Bangladesh, I have advocated building a digital factory—not merely supplying inexpensive labor, but producing competitive digital services, software, innovation, and intellectual value. The broader principle is applicable worldwide: every country and community should aim to turn digital access into locally relevant value creation, rather than treating technology adoption as an end in itself.

## The Diaspora Can Become an Opportunity Network

Diaspora and international professional networks are powerful, underused bridges between talent and demand. Bangladesh’s diaspora illustrates the potential, but the principle applies to migrant communities, alumni networks, professional associations, chambers of commerce and industry partnerships around the world.

A healthcare technologist in New York could mentor students in Dhaka; an engineer in California could guide a team in Nairobi; an entrepreneur in London could connect an emerging business in Manila with international customers. What matters is the organized transfer of market knowledge, trust, mentorship and access—not geography alone.

Universities, chambers of commerce, technology companies, diaspora associations, and workforce organizations should collaborate on structured mentorship, market access, and entrepreneurship initiatives. Every successful professional abroad should consider one question: Whose opportunity can I help unlock?

I have often said: Peace is not merely the absence of conflict. It is the presence of opportunity.

## A Globally Adaptable Agenda for Economic Opportunity

First, embed affordance literacy into education. Students should learn to identify problems, recognize technological possibilities, evaluate opportunities, and build solutions.

Second, connect skills initiatives to market demand. Training providers should be accountable for credible measures of employment, earnings, entrepreneurship, and sustained career progression—not merely enrollments and certificates.

Third, build local opportunity ecosystems. Universities, employers, investors, public institutions, and communities should collaborate on projects, startup pathways, apprenticeships, and new markets.

Fourth, mobilize the diaspora as an economic bridge. Professional networks can connect talent to international knowledge, customers, partnerships, and investment.

Fifth, measure economic empowerment rather than technological adoption alone. Ask not merely how many people use AI, but how many improve livelihoods, solve community problems, or create work for others.

These priorities form a model that can be implemented globally, not a claim that one program works identically everywhere. Start by mapping local needs and labor-market demand; pilot with employers and educators; measure employment, earnings, enterprise creation and inclusion; then adapt and scale. Bangladesh’s CodersTrust outcomes offer useful evidence, including an independent randomized evaluation of one women’s program, but they do not prove the same impacts will occur in every country. Ethical AI use, data protection, accessibility and fair competition must be built in from the start.

Here is my invitation to readers: This week, look closely at your school, workplace, community, relationships, and the technology already in your hands. Identify one possibility you have overlooked. It need not become a business: it might be a collaboration, discovery, friendship, improved service, or new way of learning. Explore it, take one modest step toward making it real, and ask what else could become possible. Some of our most valuable opportunities have been hidden in plain sight.

## Our Homework Has Changed

John McCarthy helped give artificial intelligence its name. Geoffrey Hinton helped build the scientific foundations that made today’s AI possible. Their legacies remind us that technology can advance faster than society’s ability to adapt.

My previous essay asked how we could protect young people from disappearing traditional jobs. Samdani’s response helped me see the complementary question: How can we help young people recognize and create the jobs, enterprises, services, and opportunities that do not yet exist?

For me, this is personal. I came from a village in Chattogram to the United States nearly four decades ago. Education, technology, and opportunities opened by others helped shape my journey. I have spent much of my professional life trying to extend similar possibilities to others.

At CodersTrust, our motto has been: “Learn a skill, the world is yours.” I still believe in that promise. But the AI era requires us to go further. Learn a skill. Recognize an opportunity. Solve a problem. Create value. Open a door for someone else.

Bangladesh has extraordinary young talent, as do communities around the world. Their shared challenge is often the distance between potential and accessible opportunity. A globally relevant model must help close that distance while respecting the economic realities of each country.

The future will not belong simply to those who have access to artificial intelligence. It will belong to those who recognize what it makes possible, turn those possibilities into human value, and ensure others can participate. That is the homework of our generation—and an assignment we cannot afford to fail.
