For years, I have heard Bangladesh described as a country blessed by its young. It is true, and it is also a countdown that most of us have never bothered to read. The clock is running, and the World Bank has just told us how much time is left.

A new World Bank report, *Demographic Change in South Asia: From Tailwinds to Headwinds for Development*, reads it for us. The question it asks is the one I have been trying to answer through CodersTrust since 2014: what happens to a country if its workforce grows faster than its jobs, and then the tailwind that carried it fades?

## The Dividend and the Clock

A demographic dividend is simple to describe. When a country has more adults of working age and fewer dependants to support, income can grow faster, provided those adults have productive work. The World Bank estimates that demographic trends have added about 0.4 percentage point a year to South Asia's growth since 2000.

That tailwind is fading. The report finds that the region's working-age share of the population will peak at about 69 percent in 2034 and then begin to fall. By its estimate the contribution of demographics to growth will shrink by three-quarters, to about 0.1 percentage point a year. The pool is also shrinking from the bottom: the population aged 15 to 24 in South Asia is projected to fall by about 15 percent between 2023 and 2050.

Bangladesh is later than India, and it would be easy to take comfort in that. The report puts Bangladesh's peak at 2045, about two decades from now. Do not take comfort. Twenty years is one generation, most of the people who will make up Bangladesh's workforce in 2045 are already alive and the choices that decide whether their numbers become a dividend are being made now, not in 2045. The report's own prescription is front-loaded action.

## Growing Old Before Growing Rich

The dividend is only half the story. The report warns that South Asia is aging faster than the average emerging economy, and at lower incomes. Bangladesh is projected to move from an aging to an aged society between 2027 and 2051. That shift begins next year. The country will complete it with a per capita income only slightly above where other emerging economies stood when they began theirs.

The report's phrase for this is "growing old before growing rich." Countries that aged earlier built pensions, health care and long-term care while they were still getting richer. Bangladesh will have to build them on a thinner cushion, while it is still trying to employ its young.

The time to build them is short. Only 15 years separate South Asia's working-age peak in 2034 from aged status in 2049. The region will make that shift in about 25 years. The report notes that it took France 126 years and the United States 72.

The comparison that stays with me is East Asia. The report notes that per capita GDP in the region grew an average of 4.3 percent a year between 1970 and 2000, with roughly a third of that growth attributed to demographic change under favorable policies. Latin America had a similar age structure and averaged 0.94 percent. The difference was not the number of young people. It was what each country did with them.

## The Jobs Are Already Falling Behind

This is where the report troubled me most. A dividend is realized only when a growing workforce is productively employed, and the evidence says the jobs are lagging. Across South Asia only 60 percent of the working-age population is employed, against 69 percent in other emerging economies. Only 34 percent work outside agriculture, against 51 percent. Among those who do have work, 88 percent are informally employed, a share higher than in any other emerging-market region.

Across Bangladesh, Bhutan, India and Nepal, formal firms grew employment by 2.7 percent a year in recent years. The average across emerging economies is 5.4 percent. In Bangladesh, small firms on average shed workers. About 80 percent of prime-age workers in Bangladesh, India and Nepal are informally employed. Informal employees in Bangladesh, India, Nepal and Sri Lanka earn 45 to 84 percent less than formal ones. Even among workers with postsecondary education, more than 60 percent in Bangladesh, India and Nepal work informally.

Look further ahead and the arithmetic gets harder. The report projects that by 2050 Bangladesh will have some 13 million more labor force participants than it would need to keep 2.5 workers for every older adult, the ratio the United States has today. It treats this as a risk: a labor supply that outpaces job creation.

## And AI Is Already Closing Doors

I wrote last month about what two pioneers of artificial intelligence are telling us about the jobs of our children. The World Bank now puts numbers on the same warning for South Asia.

Since ChatGPT was released in November 2022, job postings for occupations most exposed to AI and least complementary with it, such as call center agents and basic accounting, have fallen by about one-fifth. Young entrants face the highest risk, because they tend to start in routine tasks that are easiest to automate. In a region where so many people are close to the age of entering work, that matters more than it would anywhere else.

These are the same entry-level jobs we have encouraged a generation of Bangladeshi youth to pursue.

## What We Must Do Now

I am not a demographer. I have spent four decades in technology, and for the past twelve years I have led CodersTrust, which trains and mentors educated but unemployed and underemployed young people for digital work and connects them to local, remote and global work. So I read the report as a practitioner. Here is what the work has shown so far, and why I do not think we can wait.

CodersTrust has trained more than 130,000 learners since 2014. Among completers we surveyed in October 2026, 78.2 percent were employed, up from 48.0 percent before training. Median monthly income was $245; the median before training was $98. Those are before-and-after figures from survey respondents with no comparison group, so I do not offer them as proof. The evidence I trust more is a randomized controlled trial of our Women's Skill Development for Freelancing Marketplace program, run by the BRAC Institute of Governance and Development (BIGD): women offered a place were 10.3 percentage points more likely to be employed than the control group, and had an estimated 41 percent higher monthly income.

Three things follow, and none of them can wait for the next budget cycle.

**First, train now for the work that is growing, not the work that is disappearing.** Skills that complement AI hold their value, and skills that merely compete with it do not. Every course we build has to pass that test.

**Second, bring women in now.** The report notes that only 35 percent of South Asia's female working-age population is in the labor force. In the report's model, moderate improvements in schooling, learning and health, combined with rising female participation, lift Bangladesh's per capita GDP by 4 percent by 2050 compared with carrying on as today. In a more ambitious scenario that fully closes the gender gap in participation, the gain rises to 13 percent.

**Third, build the road from training to work now.** A certificate is not a job. The route runs through mentoring, through employers who will actually hire and through remote and global work that lets a young person in Barishal or Dhaka earn from markets beyond the reach of a weak local economy. Skills without that route are only a hope.

## The Window Is Open, but Not for Long

The report says that for South Asia as a whole, less than a decade remains to deepen the gains from the first demographic dividend. Bangladesh's calendar is longer, but the pressure is on us today: jobs are already growing more slowly than the workforce, AI is already closing the entry-level doors and the aging clock starts next year.

Twenty years is one generation, and a generation is not a plan. Every cohort that leaves school for an economy without jobs for it is a piece of the dividend we do not get back. We can spend the next five years building skills, mentoring and routes to work at scale, or we can spend them waiting. Bangladesh will age either way. The only choice is whether it ages with a workforce that is skilled, employed and earning or with one that was young once and never got its chance.
