The man who named artificial intelligence and the man who made it work have, between them, handed my generation a warning and a homework assignment. I want to talk about both, because the young people who will pay for our answer are the ones I have spent the last decade trying to put to work.

## The Father: When It Works, We Stop Noticing

John McCarthy coined the term "artificial intelligence" in his 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth summer workshop. I have a small personal connection to that history: years later I sat in classrooms at Dartmouth's Tuck School of Business, a few hundred yards from where the field was born.

McCarthy is credited with a wry observation that has aged remarkably well: **"As soon as it works, no one calls it AI anymore."** Spell-check, GPS, fraud alerts on your credit card, the recommendation engine on your phone. Each was once "AI." Today it is simply infrastructure.

That is the first lesson for anyone planning a young person's career. The AI that will reshape jobs in Dhaka, Chattogram and the Bronx will not arrive with a press release. It will quietly become part of the software every employer already uses. By the time a ministry announces an AI strategy, the work will already have changed.

## The Godfather: "mundane intellectual labor" Is Exposed

Geoffrey Hinton, the Nobel laureate whose work on neural networks made today's AI possible, left Google in 2023 so he could speak freely about the risks. On jobs, he has been blunt: **"For mundane intellectual labor, AI is just going to replace everybody."**

Hinton is not predicting that every office goes empty overnight. His point is sharper: a single worker equipped with an AI assistant can now do what used to take ten. He has said he would be "terrified" to work in a call center or as a paralegal today. His career advice for young people is to choose work that requires physical skill: **"Train to be a plumber."**

I have spent four decades in technology, starting as an electrical engineering student at City College of New York and building UTC Associates into a technology services firm that runs communications and IT systems for major utilities and public agencies. I have watched automation absorb whole categories of work before. What is different this time is the target. It is no longer the assembly line first. It is the desk.

## Why This Lands Hardest on Bangladesh

Read Hinton's list of exposed work, then look at the jobs we have encouraged Bangladeshi youth to pursue: call centers, back-office processing, data entry, routine graphic design, content writing, entry-level freelancing. That is, almost exactly, "mundane intellectual labor."

The numbers were worrying before AI entered the picture:

- Bangladesh's 2024 Labour Force Survey counted 2.62 million unemployed people, and 2.03 million of them were between 15 and 29.
- Roughly 885,000 unemployed people are university graduates, more than double the 2017 figure. Graduates now have the highest unemployment rate of any education group, at 13.5%, and 16.8% for young women graduates.
- Around 2.2 to 2.3 million young people enter the job market every year, about 750,000 of them university graduates.

Now add AI. Bangladesh is one of the world's largest suppliers of online freelance labor, with roughly 650,000 registered freelancers, and more than 80% of active freelancers are tertiary-educated, the group most exposed to generative AI. Freelancers are already reporting a contraction of around 21% in routine gigs.

And Hinton's "safe" physical work is not safe in Bangladesh either. The Centre for Policy Dialogue estimates that some 1.22 million ready-made garment jobs could be at risk from automation by 2041, with women bearing most of the loss. We are exposed on both fronts at once: the desk and the factory floor.

This is not only a Bangladesh story. The IMF estimates that roughly 1.2 billion young people will enter the labor force in developing economies over the next decade. India, Nigeria, Kenya, the Philippines and Pakistan face versions of the same squeeze. Bangladesh is simply where I see it most clearly, because it is where I come from and where I work.

## What I Have Learned from Training Young People

I was born in Chattogram, Bangladesh, and came to the United States as a young man in the mid-1980s. In 2014, my late friend Ferdinand Kjærulff and I founded CodersTrust in Copenhagen with a simple idea: talent is evenly distributed, opportunity is not. Today, from New York and with our largest operation in Bangladesh, CodersTrust trains young people in digital skills and connects them to work. Since 2014, we have trained more than 130,000 learners. Over 70% have gone on to earn a living by joining the platform economy as freelancers, advancing in their careers, or starting businesses of their own. Many of those freelancers are now exactly the workers Hinton is describing, which is why our next chapter is about helping them move from doing routine tasks to directing AI.

A decade of that work has taught me three things that matter more now than ever.

**First, certificates are not jobs.** A training program should be judged by how many graduates are earning six months later, not how many walked across a stage. If a course teaches a skill that AI can do for a few cents, it is teaching yesterday's job.

**Second, the value is moving up the stack.** The routine slice of a task gets automated; the judgment, the client relationship and the domain knowledge around it become more valuable. A young designer who only executes a brief is exposed. One who understands a client's business, uses AI to produce ten options in an hour and knows which one will sell is not.

**Third, "plumber" is a metaphor, not just a trade.** Hinton is right that physical, skilled, hands-on work is harder to automate. For Bangladesh, that means taking technical and vocational training seriously, from electricians and HVAC technicians to nurses and caregivers, for whom the WHO projects a global shortage of millions of health workers. The best opportunities will sit where hands, judgment and digital tools meet.

## The Evidence: When Training Ends in Earning

Skeptics are right to ask whether skills training actually changes lives, because most of the time it does not. A 2017 World Bank review of nine randomized vocational training trials found employment rose by an average of just 2.3 percentage points, and only three of the nine raised employment significantly at all. When BRAC University's Institute of Governance and Development (BIGD) ran a randomized controlled trial of CodersTrust's program for women, employment rose 10.3 points among those offered a place, more than four times that benchmark. Women who attended earned 54% more, were 26% more likely to be employed and 2.8 times as likely to freelance. Their households spent 43% more and fewer took out loans.

Our October 2026 survey of course completers tells the same story at scale. Employment rose from 48.0% before training to 78.2% at the October 2026 survey, and median monthly income rose from $98 to $245. Of 2014–2023 completers who had no income before the course, 70.8% (34 of 48) were earning at the survey, and about one in eight surveyed completers named freelancing for foreign clients or a job abroad as their main income source. Responses came from 58 of Bangladesh's 64 districts, from Panchagarh in the north to Cox's Bazar in the south. At national scale, the cost falls from about $500 to $300 per trainee. That is the model the AI era demands: fast, affordable, measured by earnings and built to scale. It is why we are now onboarding more than 1.5 million National University learners as the university's official training partner, and why our YouthWIDE program aims to train 1 million educated youth for digital employment by 2030, in Bangladesh and similar countries in South Asia and the Middle East and North Africa, depending on government and donor commitments.

## A Call to Action

McCarthy reminds us that AI becomes invisible once it works. Hinton reminds us that its impact on jobs will be anything but. Between those two truths is our responsibility.

To governments: fund outcomes, not enrollments, and fix the labor data. A country cannot manage what it does not measure.

To employers and development partners: co-design training with the companies that will hire, and build public-private partnerships that put AI tools in young people's hands, not only in their way.

To the diaspora: our networks, capital and experience are among Bangladesh's greatest assets. Mentor one young person. Open one door.

And to young people in Bangladesh and across the Global South: do not compete with AI at what it does cheaply. Learn to direct it. Build the skills it cannot copy. The future will not wait for permission, and neither should you.
